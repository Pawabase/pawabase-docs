| Model | Table | Unique on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Permission` | `permissions` | the name |
| `Group` | `perm_groups` | the name |
| `UserPermission` | `user_permissions` | — |
| `UserGroup` | `perm_user_groups` | `(user_id, group)` |
| `GroupPermission` | `perm_group_permissions` | `(group, permission)` |

All five use a ULID primary key rather than an auto-increment integer. The same module also re-keys `JWTToken` to `jwt_tokens` and `TokenBlacklist` to `token_blacklist`.

### No deny rules

Nothing in the model subtracts a permission. If you need an exception, you have three honest options:

| Approach | Trade-off |
| --- | --- |
| Do not grant the broad permission; grant a narrower one. | Best, but requires designing the permission set well. |
| Use a policy condition alongside the permission. | Fine — the permission grants the capability, the condition constrains it. |
| Add a condition that excludes the exception. | Works, and grows unwieldy as exceptions accumulate. |

Do not rely on an ordering of roles to subtract anything, because there is no ordering and no subtraction.

### Revocation is not instant

Because `roles` and `perms` are snapshotted at issue, removing a permission does not affect an access token that is already in flight. With the default 15-minute access lifetime, that is the worst-case window.

For an urgent revocation, revoke the sessions rather than only editing the role:

| Scope | How |
| --- | --- |
| One session | `DELETE /auth/v1/sessions/{session_id}` |
| Every session, by the person | `POST /auth/v1/logout` with `scope` set to `global` |
| Every session, by an operator | **Signed out everywhere** in the user view |

<Note>
Revoking a session invalidates the refresh token as well, so the access token's remaining minutes are the only window left. After that the person cannot mint a new one.
</Note>


## Choosing a model for your product

The model is the same everywhere, so the choice is not about the system. It is about how much ceremony your team wants.

### Just direct grants

Grant each person what they need, one permission at a time. Simple to operate, and the only option that needs no role design at all.

It works well for small teams and internal tools. It degrades badly at any scale, because there is no way to ask who holds a permission, and every new person is an exercise in archaeology.

### Roles with a small permission set

A handful of permissions, grouped into a handful of roles, assigned per cohort. This is the model the system is built around and the one Studio is designed for.

The cost is designing the permission set well. Names that are too coarse (`write`) force policies to add conditions; names that are too fine (`expenses:approve`) multiply the work of onboarding.

| Too coarse | Just right | Too fine |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `write` | `expenses:approve` | `expenses:approve_pending` |
| — | `reports:export` | `reports:export_csv` |
| — | `posts:publish` | `posts:publish_draft` |

The middle column is the useful one, because each name is a thing the product actually does and a policy can test it alone.

### Roles plus a wildcard for support staff

The usual shape in practice: explicit permissions for everyone, and a single role holding `*` for the two or three people who need to fix anything.

<Warning>
A wildcard role is convenient right up until a permission is added that nobody wanted the support team to have. Everything added after that is granted to them automatically. Reserve it, document who holds it, and review it when the team changes.
</Warning>

### Organizing by organization instead

If your product's real boundary is the organization rather than the job function, lean on the organization model and keep roles few: one role, or none at all, with the organization condition doing the work.

That is a legitimate design. It simply means your policies carry conditions rather than permissions, and a person who belongs to two organizations gets the union of what each allows.

| If your boundary is | Lean on | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job function. | Permissions and roles. | The capability is the same wherever the person works. |
| Organization membership. | The organization condition. | Access follows the membership, not the job. |
| Both. | Both, together. | Permission for the capability, condition for the scope. |

The last row is where most real products end up, and it is why the two systems are separate rather than merged.


## Operating roles day to day

The recurring work, and the small number of habits that keep it clean.

### Habits worth keeping

| Habit | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Define a role before assigning it. | Assigning an undefined name creates an empty role rather than an error. |
| Read a role's permission badges after saving. | A typo creates a real permission that nobody holds. |
| Check the Members column after a role change. | A role whose count drops to zero is being removed from everyone at once. |
| Revoke sessions after an urgent removal. | Claims are a snapshot; editing a role alone is not enough. |
| Keep `*` to one role, with a documented holder. | A wildcard grants permissions that do not exist yet. |

The first is the one people skip, and it is the one that produces the confusing artefacts. Defining the role is a separate, deliberate act; assigning it is not.

### Auditing access

There is no built-in "who has permission X" query, so an audit is a join or a loop. The useful shape is: for each environment, list the roles with their permissions and counts, then list the users with their roles and direct grants.

That gives you two questions answered at once: who holds a capability, and what a given person can do. Anything needing both answers — "who can export reports, and who else can do what they can" — needs the second list cross-referenced.

<Warning>
Include the wildcard when auditing. A role whose permission list contains `*` matches every capability, so a per-permission report that only counts exact names will miss it entirely.
</Warning>

### Offboarding

The order matters, because the last step invalidates everything before it.

| Order | Action | Why here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Revoke the person's sessions. | Stops the access immediately, whatever happens next. |
| 2 | Remove their roles and direct grants. | Cleans up the records. |
| 3 | Disable the account. | Stops any future sign-in. |
| 4 | Delete the account, if that is the policy. | Releases the address and removes the row from queries. |

Step 1 before step 2 because the access is in the token, not in the tables. Editing the grants first leaves up to fifteen minutes of access that the tables no longer justify.

<Note>
Deleting is a soft delete: the row is stamped, the address and username are rewritten to reserved values derived from the user's id, and identities and MFA factors are removed. The address is released for re-use; the history stays attributable.
</Note>

### The service-key question

Some recurring tasks sound like they need a service key and do not. Most of the identity surface is reachable with a publishable key and a real session, and using a service key changes what you are testing.

| Task | Needs a service key? |
| --- | --- |
| Creating a role. | Yes. The admin routes require one. |
| Assigning a role to a person. | Yes, through the admin route. |
| Reading your own resolved permissions. | No. |
| Testing whether a policy allows an action. | No, and using one will mislead you. |

<Warning>
A service credential satisfies conditions a user would not, so a green test against a policy using one proves nothing about the people you actually built it for.
</Warning>


## Migrating an authorization model

Moving from something else to this model, and moving between environments, both have sharp edges that are worth planning for.

### Coming from a flat permission list

If you arrived with a single list of permissions on a user, the direct-grant path already exists and needs no migration: each entry becomes a `UserPermission` row, and every service can enforce it the moment the API reports it.

What direct grants do not give you is a way to answer "who has this?". There is no reverse lookup from a permission to the people holding it, so an audit means iterating users rather than iterating permissions.

| Need | Direct grants | Roles |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One person, one extra capability. | Yes, quickly. | Overkill. |
| "Who can export reports?" | No. | Yes, via the role's member count. |
| "What can this person do?" | Yes. | Yes. |
| Onboarding a cohort. | No. | Yes, via `default_roles`. |
| Surviving a permission rename. | No. | No — both use the same strings. |

### Moving roles between environments

Roles are not part of a data promotion. Whatever you do, the sequence is: export the identities of the source environment, and restore them into the target.

| Property | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Role definitions travel in the backup. | Restoring creates them in the target. |
| Users travel in the backup, with their memberships. | Memberships are recreated in the target. |
| Direct permissions travel too. | Nothing is silently lost. |
| The target keeps its own rows. | A restore with `replace` deletes the target's roles first. |

<Warning>
A restore with `replace` removes **every** role in the target environment before rebuilding. If the target has roles that the source document does not mention, they are gone, along with their memberships. Take a backup of the target before restoring into it.
</Warning>

### Renaming a permission

There is no rename operation. A permission is a name, and the name is the key. To rename one you must create the new name, attach it where the old one was used, update every policy that references the old string, then clean up the old grant.

The order that avoids a gap:

1. Add the new permission name to every role that had the old one. Now both exist.
2. Update the policies to check the new name.
3. Confirm it works for anyone holding the role.
4. Remove the old name from the roles.
5. Revoke the old direct grants.

<Warning>
Step 2 is the one that strands people. A policy updated to a name nobody holds denies everyone from the moment it is saved, and the denial is a policy result rather than an error. Add the new name first, always.
</Warning>


## How the pieces fit together

A single request's path through the authorization system, end to end.

```text
1. The client sends a bearer token.
2. The gateway verifies it with the environment's key.
3. It reads prj and env, and refuses the token if either differs.
4. The verified claims become a principal on the request.
5. The policy engine reads roles and permissions from those claims.
6. The condition is evaluated and the request proceeds or is denied.
```

Step 3 is the tenancy rule doing its job. Step 5 is the only place permissions are read, and it reads a snapshot rather than the database. Steps 5 and 6 together are why authorization is cheap and why changes are not immediate.

<Note>
No service in this path queries `perm_*`. That is deliberate: enforcement must not depend on the identity service being available, or an outage there would become an outage everywhere.
</Note>

### Where roles are read, and where they are not

| Component | Reads roles? |
| --- | --- |
| The gateway, verifying a token. | No. It checks the signature and the environment only. |
| The policy engine. | Yes, from the claims. |
| Akountz, when issuing a token. | Yes, from the tables. |
| Akountz, when starting a session. | Yes, to record the session row. |
| Studio's user view. | Yes, from the tables, for display. |
| Studio's Roles tab. | Yes, from the tables, for editing. |

Two readers of the tables — one for display, one for enforcement input — and they can disagree for as long as a token lives. That is the whole of the staleness question.
