<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Can I use a permission name with spaces or capitals?">
    Yes. No pattern is enforced on permission names, only on role names. It would work, and it would be a bad idea — the name has to be typed identically in every role and every policy, so anything requiring care raises the chance of a mismatch.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Is there a length limit on a permission name?">
    None beyond the column's. In practice, keep them short enough to read in a badge in the Roles table.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What happens if I grant a permission to someone who already has it directly?">
    Nothing visible. The resolved list is deduplicated, and the grant table has a uniqueness constraint, so there is no second entry to see or remove.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="If I revoke a direct grant someone also gets from a role, does their access change?">
    No. Effective access is the union, so the role's copy remains. This is the usual explanation for "I removed that permission and they still have it".
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Does assigning a role send a notification?">
    No. Nothing in the role surface emits an event. If you need to tell someone their access changed, build it from your own event subscription or do it out of band.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Are role changes audited?">
    Not in the platform audit log, which covers configuration and administration rather than identity records. The tables change, and the effect appears on the next token issued.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Does the user's session survive a role change?">
    The session survives. Its access token keeps carrying the old claims until it expires, and the refresh token is unaffected — refreshing is what picks up the new state.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Why did the resolved permissions list not change after I saved the role?">
    Usually a cached profile rather than a stale permission. If you read the permissions from an access token or from a cached `/auth/v1/user` response, both are snapshots. Re-read after a refresh.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Can a role hold a permission that no policy ever checks?">
    Yes, and it will sit there indefinitely. Nothing reconciles permissions against policies, so an unused permission is invisible until someone audits.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Do I need the platform environment to have roles?">
    No. The reserved `_platform` environment holds Studio's operators and is configured separately, because the API does not host it. Your project environments have their own roles.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>

<Warning>
Test all five. A policy that passes the first and fails the second is the common case, and one that passes both but ignores the assurance level will look correct until the day it matters.
</Warning>

Step 3 is the tenancy rule doing its job. Step 5 is the only place permissions are read, and it reads a snapshot rather than the database. Steps 5 and 6 together are why authorization is cheap and why changes are not immediate.
### The order of a change that touches many people

Three roles and thirty people are about to lose a permission. The order below avoids the two failure modes that produce support tickets.

| Order | Action | What it prevents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Note who currently holds the affected roles. | Discovering afterwards that someone was affected and you did not know. |
| 2 | Add the replacement to the roles, keeping the old one. | A gap where nobody has the capability. |
| 3 | Update the policies to check the new name. | A window where the policy names something nobody holds. |
| 4 | Confirm it works for a holder of each affected role. | Discovering a typo in the new name at step 5. |
| 5 | Revoke sessions for anyone whose access must change now. | Carrying old claims for the access-token lifetime. |
| 6 | Remove the old name from the roles. | Nothing, at this point — it is the actual change. |
| 7 | Revoke the old direct grants. | Leaving a permission nothing checks, in a badge list forever. |

<Warning>
Step 5 is optional in the sense that it is not required for correctness — the change lands on the next refresh either way. It is required when someone should lose access today rather than within the access-token lifetime.
</Warning>

### The order for removing access entirely

Different shape, because there is no replacement to stage.

```text
1. Revoke the sessions        → access stops now
2. Remove roles and grants    → the records are cleaned up
3. Disable the account        → no future sign-in
```

Each step is independently useful, and doing them in this order means the access is gone before any of the tidying. Doing them in reverse leaves a window in which the records say one thing and the tokens say another, which is the hardest state to investigate afterwards.

### When a policy is the thing you are changing

The reverse consideration applies: turning a policy **on** denies everyone who lacks the permission, immediately, including your own test account if it lacks one.

| Change | Safe order |
| --- | --- |
| Adding a permission requirement. | Grant first, then enforce. |
| Removing a permission requirement. | Relax first, then remove the grant. |
| Adding an `aal2` requirement. | Enrol everyone first, then enforce. |
| Narrowing an existing condition. | Expect breakage; test with the affected group. |

<Note>
Every one of these is the same rule: change what people have before you change what is required, or change what is required before you change what people have if you want them to keep working. Decide which, deliberately.
</Note>

<Note>
No service in this path queries `perm_*`. That is deliberate: enforcement must not depend on the identity service being available, or an outage there would become an outage everywhere.
</Note>

### Where roles are read, and where they are not

| Component | Reads roles? |
| --- | --- |
| The gateway, verifying a token. | No. It checks the signature and the environment only. |
| The policy engine. | Yes, from the claims. |
| Akountz, when issuing a token. | Yes, from the tables. |
| Akountz, when starting a session. | Yes, to record the session row. |
### The one-page version

If you have read nothing else on this page, this is the whole model.

```text
A role      = a group row, plus the permission rows attached to it
Membership  = a row joining a person to a group
A grant     = a permission row joined straight to a person
Effective   = every direct grant, unioned with every group they belong to
Enforcement = the union, snapshotted into the token when it is issued
```

And the four things that surprise people:

| Surprise | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Assigning a role that does not exist creates it, empty. | The assignment path defines the group when it cannot find one. |
| No inheritance. `manager` does not include `employee`. | Groups hold permissions, not other groups. |
| No deny rules. Nothing subtracts a permission. | The model has positive grants only. |
| Changes are not instant. | The token is a snapshot, so a change lands on the next refresh. |

Everything else — the prefixing, the tables, the Studio screen — is mechanics in service of those four.

<Note>
If you are looking for a model with first-class role records and a separate assignment table, it does not exist here, and the closest mental model is "groups with permissions", which is exactly what it is called in the database.
</Note>


## Frequently asked questions

<AccordionGroup>
  <Accordion title="Is there a Role table I can query directly?">
    No. Roles are rows in `perm_groups`. Query that table, filter on the environment prefix, and strip it, which is exactly what the listing operation does.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="Can a role include another role?">
    No. Groups hold permissions only, and permissions are plain strings. To give a manager everything an employee has, put the person in both, or repeat the permissions in the manager role.
  </Accordion>
  <Accordion title="What happens to members when a role is deleted?">
    The memberships are deleted with it. Nobody is left pointing at a group that no longer exists, and nobody keeps the role's permissions.
  </Accordion>
</AccordionGroup>


## Worked numbers

A worked example, so the token contents are concrete rather than abstract.

Suppose `acme/production` has three roles and two permissions defined:

```text
acme/production/customer   → orders:read, orders:create
acme/production/staff      → orders:read, orders:update
acme/production/auditor    → orders:read
### Two ways to grant the same thing

Two people who can both export reports may have entirely different records, and neither record tells you more than the other.

```text
Person A:  member of a role holding reports:export
Person B:  direct grant of reports:export
```

Both resolve to the same entry in `perms`, so no policy can distinguish them, and neither should. The difference matters in three operational places:

| | Role | Direct grant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visible in the Roles tab. | Yes, with a member count. | No. |
| Visible on the user. | As a role badge. | In its own labelled list. |
| Changes if the role is edited. | Yes, immediately, for everyone. | No. |
| Changes if the grant is revoked. | Only for the person whose grant it is. | Yes. |

That third row is why a direct grant is often the safer place for a temporary exception: editing the role does not touch it.

<Warning>
The inverse is also true, and it is the more common problem: granting a permission directly to someone who already gets it from a role achieves nothing that survives an edit of that role, and leaves a badge suggesting the grant is load-bearing.
</Warning>

### Finding the source of an unexpected permission

When someone can do something you did not intend to allow, work backwards from the resolved list:

```text
perms contains reports:export
  → is reports:export in any role they hold?     check their roles
  → is it in their direct grants?                check the user's list
  → do they hold a role with *?                  check for the wildcard
  → is the policy checking something else?       read the condition
```

The fourth step is the one people skip. A policy can allow an action through a path you did not consider — an `any` branch, a second policy on the same operation, or a condition that passes for a reason unrelated to the permission.

<Tip>
When an action is allowed unexpectedly, check for a second policy before hunting the permission tables. Two policies on one operation is a supported arrangement, and either one alone may permit it.
</Tip>

```

Grace belongs to `customer` and `staff`, and additionally holds `reports:export` directly.

```text
Direct:   acme/production/reports:export
Via groups:
  acme/production/customer  →  acme/production/orders:read,  acme/production/orders:create
  acme/production/staff     →  acme/production/orders:read,  acme/production/orders:update
```

After prefixing is stripped and the list is deduplicated and sorted, her claims are:

```json
{
  "roles": ["customer", "staff"],
  "perms": [
    "orders:create",
    "orders:read",
    "orders:update",
    "reports:export"
  ]
}
```

Five points to draw from that.

**`orders:read` appears once.** It came from both groups. The union is deduplicated, so the size of the token does not grow with the number of routes to a permission.

**`orders:read` is absent from `auditor`'s badge list on her account but present in her permissions.** No — it is present in both places; the point is that it arrived via two groups, and nothing in the token records which.

**`reports:export` is indistinguishable from the rest.** A policy cannot tell that this one came from a direct grant rather than a role, and does not need to.

**`auditor` does not appear at all.** She is not in it. The role exists in the environment and is irrelevant to her.

**Removing `staff` changes two entries.** Her next token loses `orders:update` and drops `staff` from `roles`. It keeps `orders:read`, because `customer` also grants it — which is the behaviour people expect from roles but have to learn applies here.

### The same person, two environments

```json
{ "prj": "acme", "env": "staging", "roles": ["tester"], "perms": ["*"] }
```

and

```json
{ "prj": "acme", "env": "production", "roles": ["customer", "staff"], "perms": ["orders:create", "orders:read", "orders:update"] }
```

Same address, same installation, entirely separate worlds. The staging token is refused by production at the environment check, before any claim is read — and the production token would fail the same way in staging.

<Warning>
That is the failure mode behind "I gave them the role but it does not work". The role was assigned in staging, and the request is going to production. Compare the `prj` and `env` claims before looking at anything else.
</Warning>

### The wildcard, concretely

If `tester` held `*` instead of a list, her token would carry `perms: ["*"]` — a single entry, not an expansion. Nothing expands it at issue time. Every policy that checks a permission finds the wildcard at evaluation, which is why the token stays small and why the wildcard also satisfies permissions that do not exist yet.



## Reference summary

### Models and tables

| Model | Table | Unique on |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Permission` | `permissions` | the name |
| `Group` | `perm_groups` | the name |
| `UserPermission` | `user_permissions` | — |
| `UserGroup` | `perm_user_groups` | `(user_id, group)` |
| `GroupPermission` | `perm_group_permissions` | `(group, permission)` |

Every row above uses a ULID primary key rather than an auto-increment integer. The same module also re-keys `JWTToken` to `jwt_tokens` and `TokenBlacklist` to `token_blacklist`.

### Operations

| Operation | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Define a role | Creates or replaces the group and its permission set. |
| Delete a role | Removes memberships and the group. Permissions survive. |
| Assign a role | Adds a membership, creating the group if absent. |
| Revoke a role | Removes the membership, silently. |
| Grant permissions | Defines them if needed, then attaches directly. |
| Revoke permissions | Removes the direct grant, silently. |

### Names

| Rule | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Role name pattern | `^[a-z][a-z0-9_:-]{0,62}$`. |
| Permission name pattern | None enforced. |
| Storage form | `{project}/{env}/{name}`. |
| Returned form | `{name}`, filtered by prefix. |

### Claims

| Claim | Source | Snapshotted at |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `roles` | The person's group memberships. | Issue. |
| `perms` | Direct grants unioned with group permissions. | Issue. |
| `aal` | `aal1`, or `aal2` after MFA. | Issue. |
| `org`, `org_role` | The session's organization membership. | Issue. |

### Configuration

| Key | Default | Effect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `default_roles` | `[]` | Roles granted to every account at creation. |

That is the whole authorization surface of the environment's `auth` block. There is no policy expression syntax to learn, because policies are JSON and roles are groups.
